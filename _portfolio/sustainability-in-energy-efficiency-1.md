# Sustainability in Energy Efficiency Programs: Essay 1. The missing Link in Contractor Development

**Peter D. Pasholk**  
*October 2026*  
`Professional Essay` | `Markets and Delivery Systems`

---
Contractor development is vital to the success of energy efficiency programs. Programs that develop energy efficiency contractors are often assumed to follow a simple theory of change. First, train the contractors. Then give them the knowledge they need to participate. Participation should lead to projects, and projects lead to savings. On paper, the logic model for program change seemed reasonable:

Training → Knowledge → Participation → Projects → Savings

Training matters. Knowledge matters. Technical competence certainly matters. 

But after learning how successful market development programs are run and reflecting on my experience and education, I’ve been exploring whether this model leaves out something important. 

Contractors do not necessarily move from knowing how to participate to actually participating simply because they have received information or completed training. There is often something happening between knowledge and action that the conventional logic model does not capture.

That missing mechanism might be relationships. More specifically, it might be social capital.

I think this is worth exploring because it offers a different way to think about what contractor-development program actually produce. Instead of viewing these programs primarily as training programs, we should also view them as systems for helping organizations enter, navigate, and contribute to a broader network. These kinds of distinctions matter for how these programs are designed, staffed and measured.

## The Gap between Training and Participation
Consider a hypothetical. Imagine we trained, certified, and equipped 200 new contractors to participate in energy efficiency programs tomorrow. We would absolutely expect some increased in participation. It seems reasonable that more trained organization would create more opportunities for projects. 

But would all 200 of these newly trained organizations suddenly participate at a rate proportional to the support the received? Probably not. 

This is because participation requires more than technical knowledge. 

A contractor might know how a technology works, install it regularly and still not know how to participate in an energy efficiency program. They could even understand and meet program requirements and still not know who to contact if something goes wrong. They might complete a certification and not participate in the programs that require them. 

These are examples I’ve seen of new organizations knowing what to do to participate in an energy efficiency program without knowing how to navigate the system or even how that system creates value for them. 

This leads to a fundamental question contractor development programs are asking: 
What causes someone to move from knowing to participating?

We can think of several answers: relationship, trust, confidence, access, mentorships, risk mitigation, and institutional knowledge.

This essay series focuses on how relationships connect and activate these other possible answers as well as propose an expanded logic model for program change.

A trusted relationship can provide information. It can create access. It can make asking question easier. Contractors can self-identify mentors and transfer institutional knowledge that would otherwise take years to develop independently. This creates a network for opportunities to be referred to contractors who would have otherwise been invisible.  

Training provides knowledge.

Relationships turn that knowledge into action.
