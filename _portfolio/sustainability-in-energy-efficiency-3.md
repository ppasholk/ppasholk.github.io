This is where the theory becomes practical. Many contractors entering a development program are not beginners. 

They may already know how to run a business. They may have experienced employees, technical knowledge, equipment, customers, trade skills and determination. Many times the primary barrier is the lack of connection to the ecosystem that is operating the utility program.

For the multiple programs any given organization might participate in, they may not know:
-	Who to call
-	Which programs are best for their skills or customers
-	Which mistakes to avoid
-	How to participate so that their business grows

They may understand their own business very well while knowing very little about how the larger institutional system works. With this in mind, it can be argued that contractor development is not primarily about transferring information. It is about connecting capable organizations to a system in which their capabilities can create value. 

Using this logic, we can expand our logic model for program change.

**Relationships → Trust → Knowledge Sharing → Participation → Projects → Business Growth → Savings**

This does not replace the conventional framework; we are adding another layer. The argument is not that training does not matter; it’s that training is part of a larger mechanism.

## Reinterpreting Program Roles

Looking at contractor-development programs through this lens changes how we look at the people operating them. Different roles possess different types of capital.

The utility holds institutional authority. It influences program requirements, incentives, eligibility, and major decisions.

The implementation organization holds operational knowledge. It may have ownership of onboarding, processes, addressing contractor needs, and the practical pathways for resolving problems.

Field-based technical staff may hold a different and especially valuable form of capital. They have technical credibility, direct contact with contractors and program staff, and knowledge of what happens in the field and how to translate that back to the program. 

This means a field specialist is usually doing more than providing technical assistance. They likely function as an information broker. A new contractor who develops a trusted relationship with a field specialist gains more than technical answers. They gain access to information, institutional knowledge, referrals, and credibility.

In that sense, the specialist becomes the bridge. The same is true of program managers. 

A conventional view of program management emphasizes coordination, administration, budgeting, and escalation. Reinterpreting this role adds another perspective. Program managers are network architects. Their role is not simply to administer a program. It is also to understand who needs to be connected to whom, what needs to move across those connections, and where important gaps exist in the network. 

This can include connecting a new contractor with a technical resource, introducing businesses to one another, facilitating mentorship, identifying suppliers, or creating pathways for participants to remain connected after formal program support ends.

These activities can look incidental when viewed through a traditional program model. They look much more important when relationships are understood as part of the mechanism.

## The Program Produces a Network

What are contractor-development programs actually producing?

The traditional answer is: projects and savings. A more complete answer is: a functioning contractor network capable of navigating programs to find opportunities that create savings. 

This is why it is insufficient to view development programs as a training pipeline. It is also a network-building system. Its outputs might still include projects and energy savings, but some of the intermediate outcomes become relationships, trust, knowledge sharing, participation, and business growth. 

This could also explain why some participants seem to progress rapidly while others with similar capabilities struggle. The difference isn’t necessarily competence. It’s connectivity.

## Social Capital is not enough 

It is important to clarify that social capital is not a complete theory of contractor participation. Someone can know the right people and still lack confidence. Someone can have strong relationships and still not understand how a program actually works. Knowing an individual is not the same as knowing the system. 

Contractors still need to learn approval pathways, certification requirements, escalation processes, and program rules. There may also be an opportunity to think much more intentionally about the contractor-to-contractor relationships. 

Many programs are built around the relationship between the contractor and the program. What happens when the model becomes: Contractor ↔ Contractor instead?

Experienced contractors could potentially transfer knowledge and reputation to new organizations. Alumni networks could continue those relationships after graduation. Participants could become resources for one another rather than relying entirely on the program for support. Try to identify “super connectors” within these networks: people whose ability to introduce others, build trust, and navigate institutions gives them outsized influence within these networks.

Finally, there is power.

Social capital can help people navigate systems of power. It does not eliminate those systems. Formal authority over contracts, certifications, incentives, eligibility, and program requirements still shapes who can participate and how. That means a stronger contractor-development strategy will probably need to think about both relationships and institutional structures. 

## What would we Measure if Relationships were Part of the Product?

Most programs naturally count what is easiest to count:
-	How many contractors were trained?
-	How many completed certification?
-	How many completed program onboarding?
-	How many projects were completed?
-	How much energy was saved?

Those metrics are important. But suppose relationships are one of the mechanisms connecting these outcomes. 

Now we might want to know:
-	Do contractors know who they can call?
-	Have they accessed trusted sources of information?
-	Did they develop relationships outside of their immediate peer group?
-	Are experienced contractors helping newer ones?
-	How connected are participants to the broader program ecosystem?
-	How confident do contractors rate themselves on navigating the system?

These questions will encourage a different kind of contractor-development programming. Instead of measuring only whether an organization received training, we might also examine whether that organization became more connected to the ecosystem in which that participation occurs.

## A Different way to think about Contractor-Development Programs

If the goal of energy efficiency programs is simply to maximize project volume, concentrating resources among established performers may often be rational. But if the goal is to build a resilient and inclusive market, the objective is different.

The program has to create healthy pathways for organizations that do not yet possess the same relationships, access, or institutional knowledge as established participants. That requires intentionally building bridges rather than waiting for them to emerge naturally. It may mean treating mentorship as infrastructure. It may mean designing stronger alumni networks, or measuring contractor confidence and network connectivity, or deliberately creating contractor-to-contractor relationships.

And this may mean recognizing that some of the most important work happening inside a contractor-development program isn’t training, it’s making introductions, answering a phone call, explaining how a system works, or lending their reputation to a new business in a way that connects a contractor to an opportunity they otherwise would never have seen.

Those interactions can be easy to overlook because they are relational rather than transactional. But this is what creates a self-sustaining contractor ecosystem.

## Conclusion

Information matters, training matters, and technical competency matters. But they are not the entire mechanism that introduces a business to energy efficiency programs and transforms them into a participating contractor. Perhaps one of the most important things contractor-development programs create is the network that surrounds those capabilities.

Now we can start asking new questions. Where are bonding and bridging already happening? Where are the gaps? Who are the connectors? How do we make the introduction

I do not think social capital explains every reason one contractor succeeds and another struggles. But I do think it offers a useful lens that shifts the question from “How do we train more contractors?” to something broader:

How do we intentionally build the relationships that allow more contractors to participate, succeed, and contribute to the system?
