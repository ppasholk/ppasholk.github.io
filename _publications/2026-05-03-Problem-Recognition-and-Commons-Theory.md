excerpt: 'This paper examines how environmental problems become recognized as collective action problems in the first place, using Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring and the eventual U.S. ban on DDT as a case study. It situates Carson’s work between Garrett Hardin’s Tragedy of the Commons and Elinor Ostrom’s theory of polycentric governance, arguing that both frameworks largely assume that a commons problem has already been recognized. The paper proposes a missing pre-institutional “activation” stage centered on problem recognition, communication, and normative transformation. In doing so, it offers a revised multi-stage framework for understanding how diffuse environmental harms become collective governance problems.'
